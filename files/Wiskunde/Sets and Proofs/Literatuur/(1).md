## 2 Logically Speaking
---
An example of a valid argument:
- Suppose that "$A$ or $B$ is true", 
- and you happened to know that "$B$ is not true,"
- you would conclude that "$A$ is true."

A statement = a sentence that is true or false.

**Exercise 2.1:** Which of these are statements:
(a) "It is raining outside" is a statement
(b) "The professor is a woman" is a statement
(c) "Two plus two is five" is a statement
**(d)** $X + 6 = 0$ is not a statement ($X$ is dependent variable)
(e) "Seven is a prime number" is a statement
(f) "All odd numbers are prime" is a statement
**(g)** "This sentence is false." is not a statement (Suppose this sentence was true, then it had to be both true and false, which is impossible for a statement.)

The rules of logic should work for all statements: 
- We introduce letters (such as $P$, $Q$, $R$ and $S$) to represent statements.
- Thus $P$ will have two possible truth values (true ($T$) or false ($F$)).

Statement forms are formed when statements are combined with **logical connectives**:
- Negation: "Not $P$"
- Conjunction: "$P$ and $Q$"
- Disjunction: "$P$ or $Q$" 
- Implication: "If $P$, then $Q$"
- Equivalence: "$P$ if and only if $Q$"

| $P$ | $Q$ | $\neg Q$ | $P \lor Q$ | $P \land Q$ |
| --- | --- | -------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| $F$ | $F$ | $T$      | $F$        | $F$         |
| $F$ | $T$ | $F$      | $T$        | $F$         |
| $T$ | $F$ |          | $T$        | $F$         |
| $T$ | $T$ |          | $T$        | $T$         |

Consider the implication "If $P$, then $Q$." 
- Written as $P \rightarrow Q$
- Often stated as "$P$ implies $Q$", may also be stated as:
	- $Q$ if $P$
	- $P$ is sufficient for $Q$ (meaning $P$ is enough to make $Q$ happen)
	- $Q$ is necessary for $P$ (if $P$ happened, then $Q$ must have happened)
	- $P$ only if $Q$ (if $P$ happened, then $Q$ must have happened)
	- $Q$ whenever $P$
- $P$ is called the **antecedent** or **hypothesis**
- $Q$ is called the **conclusion** or **consequent**

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P \rightarrow Q$ |
| --- | --- | ----------------- |
| $F$ | $F$ | $T$               |
| $F$ | $T$ | $T$               |
| $T$ | $F$ | $F$               |
| $T$ | $T$ | $T$               |

Consider the equivalence "$P$ if and only if $Q$":
- Written as $P \leftrightarrow Q$
- This is the same as "($P$ only if $Q$) and ($P$ if $Q$)" $\equiv (P \rightarrow Q) \land (Q \rightarrow P)$
- The equivalence is true precisely when $P$ and $Q$ are both true or both false.

The statement forms $P \rightarrow Q$ and $P \leftrightarrow Q$ might be written as 
- $P \Rightarrow Q$
- $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ or "$P$ iff $Q$"

**Exercise 2.6:**
- $\neg ( P \lor Q) \leftrightarrow (\neg P \land \neg Q)$ is a tautology

**Definition:**
- A statement form = a letter representing an unspecified statement or expression built from such letters using connectives
- Two statement forms $P$ and $Q$ are logically equivalent if $P \rightarrow Q$ is a tautology

When we notice a statement is always true, we can state that fact as a theorem.
- We will need to give a conclusive argument (proof of the theorem)

**Theorem 2.7:** Two statement forms $P$ and $Q$ are equivalent iff they have the same truth table.

**Proof.** Consider the truth table for $P \leftrightarrow Q$:
- We know that $P \leftrightarrow Q$ is a tautology iff $P \leftrightarrow Q$ has the truth value $T$ (in row 1 and 4)
- Finally $P \leftrightarrow Q$ has the truth value $T$ if and only if $P$ and $Q$ have the same truth value
- Since $P$ and $Q$ are equivalent if and only if $P \leftrightarrow Q$ is a tautology, $P$ and $Q$ must have the same truth table.
$\square$

**Exercise 2.8:** 
- $(P \rightarrow Q) \leftrightarrow (\neg P \lor Q)$ is a tautology
- $\neg (P \rightarrow Q) \equiv (P \land \neg Q)$

It is also important to negate statements (e.g. the negation of an implication)
- "If $x$ is prime, then $x$ is odd or $x = 2$."
	- $P =$ "$x$ is prime"
	- $Q =$ "$x$ is odd or $x = 2$"
- Negating this leads to: "$x$ is prime and it is not the case that $x$ is odd or $x = 2$." 
- Negating the disjunction "$x$ is odd or $x = 2$" leads to: "$x$ is prime and $x$ is not odd and $x \neq 2$"
- Refining this further, we would say "$x$ is prime, even and not equal to two."

**Theorem 2.9:** Let $P$ and $Q$ denote statement forms. The following are tautologies:
- **(DeMorgan's laws):** 
	- $\neg (P \lor Q) \leftrightarrow (\neg P \land \neg Q)$ 
	- $\neg (P \land Q) \leftrightarrow (\neg P \lor \neg Q)$
- **(Implication and it's negation):**
	- $(P \rightarrow Q) \leftrightarrow (\neg P \lor Q)$
	- $\neg (P \rightarrow Q) \leftrightarrow (P \land \neg Q)$
- **(Double negation):** $\neg (\neg P) \leftrightarrow P$

**Exercise 2.10:** Negate the following
(a) If I go to the party, then he is there.
- $P =$ "I go to the party"
- $Q =$ "He is there."
- The negation of this statement is **"I go to the party and he is not there."**
(b) If $x$ is even, then $x$ is divisible by 2.
- $P$ = "$x$ is even"
- $Q$ = "$x$ is divisible by 2"
- The negation of this statement is **"$x$ is even and $x$ is not divisible by 2."**
(c) If a function is differentiable, then it is continuous
- $P$ = "a function is differentiable"
- $Q$ = "a function is continuous"
- The negation of this statement is **"a function is differentiable and is not continuous."**
(d) If $x$ is a natural number, then $x$ is even or $x$ is odd.
- $P$ = "$x$ is a natural number"
- $Q$ = "$x$ is even or $x$ is odd"
- The negation of this statement is $x$ is a natural number and $x$ is neither even nor $x$ is odd."
- Simplifying it further gives **"$x$ is a natural number, not even and not odd."**

**Exercise 2.11:**
- $P \rightarrow Q \leftrightarrow \neg P \lor Q$
- $\neg (P \lor Q) \leftrightarrow \neg P \land \neg Q$
- $\neg (P \land Q) \leftrightarrow \neg P \lor \neg Q$
- $\neg (P \rightarrow Q) \leftrightarrow P \land \neg Q$

Mr. French, who eats only pickles on Wednesday and only chocolate on Monday
- $W$ = "It is Wednesday" and $P$ = "Mr. French eats pickles."
- Thus we know that $W \rightarrow P$ is true.
- $M$ = "It is Monday" and $C$ = "Mr. French eats only chocolate."
- We know $M \rightarrow C$ is true.
- Finally we are told "Mr. French is eating chocolate." From this we can conclude $\neg P$ is true.

Our argument becomes:
1. $W \rightarrow P$
2. $M \rightarrow C$
3. $\neg P$

| $W$   | $P$   | $W \rightarrow P$ | $\neg P$ |
| ----- | ----- | ----------------- | -------- |
| ==F== | ==F== | ==T==             | ==T==    |
| F     | T     | T                 | F        |
| T     | F     | F                 | T        |
| T     | T     | T                 | F        |
- We know that both $W \rightarrow P$ and $\neg P$ are true when both $W$ and $P$ are false.
- All we can conclude is, that it is not Wednesday.
