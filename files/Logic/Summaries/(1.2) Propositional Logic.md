## Ch. 2.1: Propositional Logic

### Ch. 2.1.10: Classifying Propositions
---
**Definition 2.4:**
- A compound proposition is said to be a **tautology** if it is $\mathbb{T}$ for all situations
- A compound proposition is said to be a **contradiction** if it is $\mathbb{F}$ for all situations
- A compound proposition is said to be a **contingency** if it is neither a tautology nor a contradiction

Any compound proposition $P$ is a tautology if and only if $\neg P$ is a contradiction.

**Definition 2.5:** Logical equivalence can be defined in terms of tautology
- Two compound propositions, $P$ and $Q$ are **logically equivalent** if and only if the proposition $P \leftrightarrow Q$ is a tautology.

The assertion that $P$ is logically equivalent to $Q$ is expressed as:
- $P \equiv Q$

For example: 
- $p \rightarrow q \equiv (\neg p \lor q)$
- $p \oplus q \equiv (p \lor q) \land \neg (p \land q)$

## Ch. 2.3: Logic Circuits
---
### Ch. 2.3.4: Disjunctive Normal Form
---
Compound propositions correspond naturally with combinatorial logic circuits.

Given **any** truth table it is possible to find a proposition, containing only the $\land, \lor,$ and $\neg$ operators.

**A simple term** = either a propositional variable or the negation of a propositional variable.

**A conjunction of simple terms** = one or more simple terms put together with $\land$ operators.
- Such as $p \land q$, $p$, $\neg q$, $p \land \neg r \land \neg w \land s \land t$

Finally, we can take 1 or more such conjunctions into a "disjunction of conjunctions of simple terms".

Some rules to avoid redundancy:
1. Propositional variables do not occur more than once in a single conjunction.
2. The same conjunction does not occur twice in the disjunction

**Definition 2.6:**
- A compound proposition is said to be in **disjunctive normal form**, or DNF, if it is a disjunction of conjunctions of simple terms.
- If each propositional variable occurs at most once in each conjunction
- And if each conjunction occurs at most once in the disjunction

**Theorem 2.3:**
- Consider a table that lists a logical output value for every situation of several propositional variables
- Assume at least one of the output values is true ($\mathbb{T}$)
- Then there is a proposition containing those variables  s.t. the value of the proposition for each situation is precisely the value specified in the table
- It is possible to choose the proposition to be in DNF

**How to find DNF form of a function:**
1. Consider any row in the table for which the output value is $\mathbb{T}$
2. Form a conjunction of simple terms as follows:
	- If a variable, $p$, is $\mathbb{T}$ in that row, include $p$ itself in the conjunction
	- If a variable $q$, is $\mathbb{F}$ in that row, include $\neg q$ in the conjunction
	- The value of this conjunction is $\mathbb{T}$, since each of the terms in the conjunction is true for that combination of variables.
	- For any other combination of variable values, the value will be $\mathbb{F}$, since at least one of the simple terms in the conjunction will be false.
3. Take the disjunction of all such conjunctions constructed in this way.

This disjunction has the value $\mathbb{T}$ if and only if one of the conjunctions has the value $\mathbb{T}$, and that is when the output value specified by the table is $\mathbb{T}$

| $p$          | $q$          | $r$          | $\text{output}$ |                                 |
| ------------ | ------------ | ------------ | --------------- | ------------------------------- |
| $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$    |                                 |
| $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{T}$    | $(\neg p \land \neg q \land r)$ |
| $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$    |                                 |
| $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{T}$    | $(\neg p \land q \land r)$      |
| $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$    |                                 |
| $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{F}$    |                                 |
| $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{F}$ | $\mathbb{F}$    |                                 |
| $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{T}$ | $\mathbb{T}$    | $(p \land q \land r)$           |

### Translating to propositional calculus
---
My program is correct or it does not terminate
- $c =$ 'my program is correct'
- $t =$ 'my program terminates' 
$$c \lor \neg t \equiv  t \rightarrow c \equiv  \neg c \rightarrow \neg t \equiv \neg (t \land \neg c)$$

My pc has no problems if I do not use windows
- $p =$ 'my pc has problems'
- $w =$ 'I use windows'
$$\neg w \rightarrow \neg p \equiv p \rightarrow w \equiv \neg p \lor w$$

### Normal forms
---
**Definition:** Literals
- A **literal** is a propositional variable or the negation of it: $p, \neg p, q, \neg s, ...$

**Theorem**: 
- Every formula in propositional calculus can be rewritten to DNF, a disjunction of conjunctions of literals.

**Q:** Rewrite this formula to DNF: $\neg ((p \land q) \rightarrow \neg(r \rightarrow q))$

### Complementary literals in DNF
---
**Definition:** Complementary literals
- Two literals are called **complementary** iff one is the negation of the other
- $(p, \neg p), (q, \neg q), ...$

**Q:** If in each conjunction of a DNF formula $F$ there are complementary literals, what does that mean for $F$.
**A:** $F$ is a contradiction (i.e. always $\mathbb{F}$)

### Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF)
---
CNF is the **dual** of DNF

**Theorem:** Conjunctive Normal Form
- Every formula in propositional calculus can be rewritten to CNF, a conjunction of disjunction of literals.
- For instance: $(p \lor \neg q) \land (q \lor \neg r \lor \neg s) \land (\neg p \lor r) \land ...$

**Q:** If in each disjunction of a formula in CNF there are complementary literals, what do we know about the formula?
**A:** The formula is a tautology

### Introduction to First-Order Logic
---
**Q:** Is the following true?
$$ \text{For all real numbers } x, \text{ if } x \gt 10, \text{ then } x \gt 5$$
**A:** Yes, the statement is true:
- $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}, (x > 10 \rightarrow x > 5)$
- Any real number greater than 10 is automatically greater than 5.

**Q:** What is the negation of the following sentence? 
$$
\text{"All students from Delft have a bicycle."}
$$
**A:** Answer C, "There exists a student from Delft without a bicycle."

**Q:** When does the following statement become false?
$$
\text{"All students from Delft have a bicycle."}
$$
**A:** Answer F, "Every student from Delft has no bicycle and no car."

**Q:** When does the following statement become false?
$$
\begin{align*}
\text{"If all students in Delft have a bicycle,} \\
\text{then all students in Delft have a car."}
\end{align*}
$$
**A:** ??

**Q:** Give a formula that translates $\text{"All prime numbers are even."}$, where:
- $E(x) =$ '$x$ is even'
- $P(x) =$ '$x$ is a prime number'
**A:** $\forall x(P(x) \rightarrow E(x))$

**Q:** Give a formula that translates $\text{"There exists an even prime number"}
**A:** $\exists x (P(x) \land (E(x)))$
